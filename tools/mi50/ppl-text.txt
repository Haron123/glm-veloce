The history of computing machinery is, in large part, a history of trade-offs between
speed, capacity, and cost. The earliest electronic computers filled entire rooms and
consumed as much power as a small factory, yet they performed fewer arithmetic
operations per second than a modern wristwatch. Their designers faced constraints that
would be unrecognizable today: memory was measured in words rather than gigabytes, and
every instruction had to be counted, because machine time was the most expensive
resource in the laboratory.

The invention of the transistor changed the economics of computation. Where a vacuum
tube consumed watts and failed after a few thousand hours, a transistor consumed
milliwatts and could be expected to outlive the machine around it. Integrated circuits
carried the trend further, packing first dozens, then thousands, then billions of
devices onto a single piece of silicon. Gordon Moore observed in 1965 that the number
of components per integrated circuit seemed to double every year, and the industry
proceeded to follow that observation, later called Moore's Law, for half a century.

Memory technologies followed their own path. Magnetic cores, small rings of ferrite
threaded onto wire grids, gave way to semiconductor memories in the early 1970s.
Dynamic random access memory stored each bit as a charge on a capacitor, a scheme so
simple and dense that it still dominates main memory today. Static random access
memory, faster but hungrier, found its niche in caches close to the processor. The
layering of these memories, small and fast near the core, large and slow farther away,
became the memory hierarchy that every modern computer obeys.

Graphics processors began as fixed-function helpers, drawing shaded triangles into a
frame buffer. As the demand for realism grew, the pipelines became programmable, and
by the middle of the 2000s researchers realized that the hardware was, at heart, a
large array of simple arithmetic units controlled by software. General purpose
computing on graphics hardware was born, and with it a new style of programming: the
same instruction executed across thousands of data elements, with performance
depending less on the speed of a single calculation than on how efficiently data
could be moved to the units that consumed it.

The economics of bandwidth deserve their own chapter. A processor can perform
arithmetic far faster than it can fetch the operands from main memory. A cache miss
can cost as much time as several hundred floating point operations, so the
programmer's first duty is often not to compute less but to move less. Locality of
reference, blocking, tiling, and prefetching are the vocabulary of this discipline.
Kernels that stream over their data exactly once, touching each element a single
time, approach the speed of the memory system itself; kernels that revisit memory
haphazardly spend their lives waiting.

Quantization addresses a different cost: the size of the model itself. A neural
network trained in thirty-two bit floating point can often be served in eight bit
integers with little measurable loss, because the network's behavior is dominated by
a few strong signals rather than by the fine grain of its weights. The art lies in
choosing the scale factors: per block of weights, per channel, per tensor. More
granularity preserves accuracy but adds overhead to every computation. The most
aggressive schemes pack two or three bits per weight, trading careful bookkeeping
for the ability to hold a model that would otherwise not fit in memory at all.

Inference with large language models is a study in two regimes. When a prompt is
processed, many tokens pass through the network at once, and the arithmetic units
are saturated; throughput is bounded by how fast the weights can be streamed. When
the model generates, a single token passes through at a time, and the same weights
are read again and again for a tiny amount of work per element; the machine is
latency bound, and the cost of launching each small computation matters as much as
the computation itself. Engines that serve these models therefore maintain two sets
of kernels, one for each regime, and choose between them by the shape of the batch.

Speculative decoding exploits the asymmetry between the two regimes. A small draft
model, or a lightweight head attached to the main one, proposes several tokens at
low cost; the large model then verifies all of them in a single batched pass,
accepting the longest correct prefix. Because verification runs in the throughput
regime while plain generation runs in the latency regime, several tokens can be
confirmed for little more than the cost of one. The output distribution is provably
unchanged, which is what makes the technique safe: the model says exactly what it
would have said, only sooner.

The parallel machines that host these workloads exchange partial results across
wires. Where the wires are fast, the exchange is a formality; where they are slow,
it is the central problem of the design. Engineers speak of all-reduce, the
operation in which every participant contributes a fragment and every participant
receives the sum. The naive implementation sends everything to one place, sums it,
and sends it back out again. Better implementations arrange the participants in
rings and butterflies, so that each one touches the network a bounded number of
times. The best implementations fuse the exchange into the computation itself, so
that no fragment is ever written to memory that another device will not read.

Cooling is the quiet constraint. A data center accelerator that draws three hundred
watts must shed three hundred watts, continuously, into air that someone must then
move. Passive coolers rely on airflow through the chassis; a card that idles at
thirty degrees in a cold room may throttle at ninety in a warm one, and the
performance figures quoted in specifications are always measured at temperatures
that a careless installer will never see. The operator's checklist is short: feed
the front with cold air, exhaust the back, and never stack cards without a gap.

Documentation is part of the machine. A kernel that cannot be understood cannot be
tuned, and a benchmark that cannot be reproduced cannot be trusted. The discipline
of writing down what was measured, on what hardware, with what version, is what
separates an engineering project from a collection of anecdotes. Numbers age, but
the habit of recording them does not, and the person who returns to a project after
a year will thank the one who wrote down not only the results but the method.
